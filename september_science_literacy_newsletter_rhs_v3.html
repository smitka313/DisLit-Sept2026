What do we actually know from the evidence?

Our opening units give students plenty to look at: maps, readings, lab observations, data, simulations, and models. A useful move this month is simply slowing students down before they jump to an explanation. What do they actually see in the evidence, and what are they inferring from it?

Move #1: Evidence first. Explanation second.

Before students explain a graph, reading, lab result, photo, or simulation, ask them to separate what the source shows from what they think it means.

A question to steal
“What can we say for sure from this evidence before we try to explain it?”

This can be as simple as two columns in a notebook: What we see / What we think it means.

Move #2: Make the evidence behind a model visible.

When students change a model, ask them to point to the thing that made them change it. That turns model revision from “fixing the picture” into showing how their thinking changed.

A question to steal
“What did you change, and what did we see or learn that made you change it?”

Quick option: have students add one “Changed because…” note next to a revision.

Coming up: places to try it

I looked a few lessons ahead in each of our opening units. These aren't extra activities. They're spots already in the curriculum where one of the moves above fits pretty naturally.

Biology • B2around Lesson 2

Students investigate peat and why it burns, including observations from burning fuels and information about peat/permafrost. This is a nice spot to keep observations and explanations from blending together too quickly.

Try: Before discussing why peat releases so much CO₂, ask, “What did we actually observe or measure? What are we using that evidence to explain?”

Chemistry • C1Lessons 2–3

Students work with the “Year Without a Summer” reading and then atmospheric CO₂ information as they build the sea-level-rise storyline. There are multiple sources on the table, which makes this a good chance to talk about what each source can — and can't — tell us.

Try: “Which piece of evidence is doing the most work for your idea right now? What does it support, and what doesn't it prove yet?”

Physics • P1around Lessons 2–3

Students trace the energy infrastructure around them and start figuring out how electricity gets from where it's produced to where it's used. Their system models are going to change as they uncover more of the story.

Try: When students add something to a system model, have them tag one addition with: “We added this because…” and name the observation, reading, or investigation behind it.

Weather • 6.3Lesson 2

This one is almost ready-made. Students analyze hailstone images, a hail-frequency map, and weather data from hailstorms. The lesson even has students mark “What I See” in the data.

Try: After the “What I See” step, add: “So what do you think that might mean?” Then ask students to be clear about where the evidence ends and the inference begins.

Interactions • Unit 1Investigation 1

Students test how tape, balloons, rods, and other charged objects interact, then use those observations to build and revise a model of electrostatic interactions. Evidence → model revision is already the point of the investigation.

Try: Before a model revision, ask each group to name the one observation that most needs to be accounted for in their new model. Then make sure the revision actually accounts for it.

Four questions worth stealing this month

  • What do we actually see in the evidence?
  • What are we inferring from it?
  • What changed in your thinking?
  • What evidence made you change it?
Science Literacy: Part of the Process is meant to highlight useful literacy moves inside the science we're already teaching — not add another thing to the plate.

Curriculum references: OpenSciEd B.2, C.1, P.1, and 6.3 unit materials; CREATE for STEM Interactions Unit 1.
